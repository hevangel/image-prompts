## The prompt

The source set's author comment carries the creator's full GPT prompt
(written in Chinese, with a Chinese introduction). The creator
publishes it for followers to copy. The full text lives in that
comment, linked above; what follows is a structural summary.

The prompt asks for each uploaded photo to become its own independent
high-end design poster, never splicing photos, on a 3:4 canvas in two
zones strictly 1:1. The top zone preserves the original photo
faithfully with only slight high-end grading; the environment may
extend naturally but the subject is never stretched, distorted or
changed. The bottom zone does not redraw the photo: it first
understands the original's meaning, emotion, symbolism, cultural
associations, psychological tension and deeper metaphors, and from the
subject relationships, actions, distances, directions, colors and
accidental details distills the most memorable, poetic visual moment,
rebuilding only that from Chinese porcelain shards of varied sizes and
irregular shapes — deleting irrelevant detail and keeping only
contours, structure, movement and visual memory points. The shards
must show real glaze surfaces, ceramic-body fracture edges, cracks,
chipped corners and hints of traditional pattern; glazes are chosen
flexibly per the photo's temperament (blue-and-white, celadon,
famille-rose, monochrome glazes, underglaze red), with pattern only as
a local hint, never upstaging the subject. Gaps, misalignment and
incompleteness between shards are allowed — breaking and
re-aggregating is itself part of the narrative. The composition is a
small-scale seal-like subject with large white space, placed freely by
the subject's direction, proportion and visual center (off-center,
edge-touching, floating, partly cropped); the frame is never filled;
the subject may show only its most crucial part; the blank actively
joins the positive/negative shapes, distance, pauses and spatial
relations so the shards' gathering and gaps create breathing room,
lingering resonance and psychological tension. Color: the most vivid,
memorable colors of the photo above, translated into porcelain glaze
and pattern colors — never a mechanically fixed blue-and-white blue;
clear, bright, real glaze luster without excess opulence; refined
photo-derived colors balanced by large areas of warm porcelain white
or a very pale ground. Text is minimal editorial intervention only,
language unrestricted, with no preset titles/numbering: a few words or
a short phrase distilled from the photo's theme, emotion, cultural
associations or metaphor, placed in the white space, at shard edges or
between visual relationships so text and subject complete the
composition together. Overall: not a porcelain-shard version of the
photo but a re-creation after understanding it — porcelain shards as
the medium expressing the original's spirit through breakage, loss,
reassembly, white space and cultural memory; poetic, restrained,
contemporary, Eastern, psychologically tense, collectible. Excluded:
object-by-object replication, filled frames, heaped traditional
pattern, antique-display feel, cheap "national-trend" feel, 3D plastic
feel, templated collage.

> Full prompt text: see the source set's author comment (linked above).

## English translation of the introduction

The caption (Traditional Chinese) says this time he turns photos into
broken-porcelain collage: let AI understand which moment in the photo
is worth keeping, then "shatter" that moment and reassemble it from
porcelain. The top half keeps the original photo; the bottom half does
not redraw the photo at all — it may keep a tunnel-mouth landscape,
points of light in a night scene, a building's outline, a doll's gaze,
or even just one accidental little detail — then rebuilds those most
memorable parts from porcelain shards of different sizes. There is
blue-and-white, celadon and famille-rose, maybe underglaze red, but the
colors are not a fixed "Chinese-style" overlay — they are re-taken
from the original photo. Cracks, chipped corners and blanks are
deliberately kept; some shards don't quite join; some spots are even
deliberately missing a piece, giving an ordinary photo a "broken,
then pieced back together" feeling — and every photo's result really
does come out different. The full instruction is in the images / first
comment.
