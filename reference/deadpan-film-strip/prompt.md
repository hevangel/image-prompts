## The prompt

The full prompt sits in the post body (a long detailed version) and, in a shorter condensed form, in the author's first comment on the set post. The creator publishes it for followers to copy. The full text lives there, linked above; what follows is a structural summary.

The prompt turns each uploaded photograph into its own independent "Original Photography × Deadpan Chibi Film Strip" comparison poster on a strict vertical 3:4 canvas, split horizontally into two seamless equal 50% sections — no text, borders, labels, logos or watermarks:

- **Top half — original photography.** A faithful copy of the original photo — subjects, faces, clothing, poses, relationships, composition, lighting and colours — with no changes beyond minimal proportional cropping or natural background extension.
- **Bottom half — deadpan chibi film strip.** Auto-detects the main people, visible pets and a curated selection of the most recognizable objects actually present in the photo, and reinterprets each as one tiny, bored, hand-drawn 2–3-head-tall chibi character with tired fish eyes, half-closed eyelids, subtly awkward poses, relaxed Japanese retro hand-drawn ink outlines, imperfect organic flat colour blocks, slight print-registration offsets and subtle paper grain — no gradients or realistic shading. Exactly one subject per occupied frame of a single horizontal retro film strip centred in the bottom half, occupying about 35% of its height, surrounded by generous clean negative space. Objects are personified using their original shapes and colours as their bodies, adding only tiny deadpan eyes, a flat mouth and short stubby limbs — never separate human bodies.
- **Frame count and order.** 5, 7 or 9 film frames according to the available subjects; genuinely empty frames are allowed rather than inventing extra characters, and there is exactly one central frame. The main subject sits in the slightly enlarged centre frame, with the surrounding subjects following their original relative visual order as closely as possible; frames gradually become slightly smaller toward both ends.
- **Colour rule.** The centre frame is the ONLY full-colour frame, using its subject's authentic colours; all other characters fade progressively into paler grey or muted tones toward the outer edges, while every frame keeps consistently dark, readable black outlines.
- **Film-strip dressing.** Thin, slightly wobbly hand-drawn film borders, sprocket holes along both edges of the strip, and a clean near-white paper background subtly tinted toward 1–2 authentic colours extracted from the source photo. Each occupied frame is free of backgrounds and unrelated props.
- **Mood.** Minimal, tender, nostalgic, gently absurd, quietly funny. No invented subjects, no extra objects, no decorative scenery, no additional strips or panels.
- **Tip in the caption.** After generating, check which object's deadpan expression is most interesting, and ask the AI to fine-tune if the person count, object shapes or film-frame count drift.

## English translation of the introduction

The caption (Traditional Chinese) opens by saying that before, people zoned out together — now even the whole world has gone world-weary with them — and explains that this extends the earlier deadpan Q-version prompt into a new game: the previous version turned people and pets into short-limbed, half-lidded, don't-want-to-work chibi characters, and now even the coffee cup, chair and potted plant in a photo can grow their own world-weary faces. It gives examples: the coffee cup half-closes its eyes as if not yet awake; the potted plant droops as if even sunbathing is too much effort; the chair grows short little limbs and looks like it wants a rest more than the person sitting on it; the backpack looks utterly done with life, as if carrying the world's worries. Keeping each object's original shape and adding only simple eyes, a mouth and short limbs is enough to give it its own little mood. The retro film-strip design gives every character its own frame in a slightly absurd, inexplicably cute world-weary squad, with a hidden touch: the middle protagonist keeps its full original colours while the others fade toward the sides, as if each person and object had its own little camera yet were all acting in the same plotless world-weary short film. If a photo doesn't have enough characters, the strip keeps blank frames rather than stuffing in things that weren't there. It closes by asking readers which everyday object growing a deadpan face would break down first — the alarm clock that wakes you, or the backpack full of work — and invites them to share their results.
