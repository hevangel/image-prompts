## The prompt

The full prompt sits in the post caption and identically in the author's pinned first comment on the set post. The creator publishes it for followers to copy. The full text lives there, linked above; what follows is a structural summary.

The prompt turns each uploaded photograph, separately and in upload order (never mixing subjects or elements between photos), into its own independent "Original Photography × Expressive Cubist Modern Art" comparison poster on a strict 3:4 portrait canvas, split horizontally into exactly two equal 50% halves, seamlessly joined — no borders, dividers, text, logos or watermarks:

- **Top half — original photography.** A faithful copy of the original photo — identity, facial features, expressions, poses, clothing, composition, important objects, natural lighting, textures and original colours — with only subtle editorial colour correction, minimal cropping or natural background extension.
- **Bottom half — expressive Cubist artwork.** The same complete scene reinterpreted as a sophisticated hand-painted Cubist artwork in Pablo Picasso's expressive modernist visual language, keeping the subjects, silhouettes, gestures, objects, spatial relationships and mood recognisable, simplified into about 5–12 large cohesive structural masses with bold colour blocking, sweeping organic curves, irregular geometric forms, asymmetrical shapes and selective expressive distortion (familiar figures may become slightly exaggerated, e.g. the eyes). Strong, slightly imperfect black/charcoal hand-painted contours only on essential silhouettes and boundaries; minimal internal segmentation, generous negative space, large uninterrupted colour fields, a clear focal point; backgrounds simplified into a few quiet abstract shapes. Palette of about 4–6 expressive colours drawn from the photo's dominant colours, with selective warm–cool contrast; subtle brushstrokes, imperfect painted edges, matte pigments, restrained canvas texture. Recognisable facial features are preserved when visible; never invent hidden features, people, objects or scenery. Adapts naturally to portraits, animals, architecture, landscapes, food and still life.
- **Avoid.** Excessive fragmentation, tiny polygons, geometric grids, mosaics, stained-glass patterns, checkerboards, low-poly effects, glossy digital gradients, cartoon aesthetics — and never cut the picture up so finely or messily that it ends up like a jigsaw puzzle and loses its overall beauty.
- **Priorities / final check.** Monumental shapes, organic movement, expressive simplicity, authentic fine-art painting; exact 3:4 format, strict 50/50 horizontal split, faithful original above, clearly recognisable expressive Cubist painting below, broad colour planes, minimal dividing lines, strong contours, refined museum-quality aesthetic.

## English translation of the introduction

What would your photo look like if it were redrawn in Picasso's style? This time I tried turning ordinary photos into something bold and full of character — a Cubist art feel. Placing the top and bottom halves together, the contrast is quite interesting! The lower half uses just a few large blocks of colour and a few lines, yet they can be arranged into all kinds of different feelings. My favourite part is those large areas of colour, paired with rough, characterful lines. Familiar figures may become a little exaggerated (the eyes, for example); fine-grained scenery is simplified into different shapes and colours. But the interesting thing is that these seemingly simple changes actually give the whole picture more power, and more of a hand-drawn art feel. This prompt also makes a special point of not cutting the picture up too finely or messily, so it doesn't end up like a jigsaw puzzle and lose its overall beauty. Whether people, architecture, or travel scenery, all of it can be reinterpreted through AI. It takes the original outlines, movements, colours and scenes as reference, then recombines them with large colour blocks and lines into a different picture. Although the fine detail of the real photo is gone, you can still recognise the familiar scene. Sometimes artistry isn't about how finely you paint, but about how few simple lines it takes to capture what makes a photo distinctive.

(Then the creator asks readers which of the nine works they like best and invites them to say so in the comments.)
